\chapter{Conclusions}

\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}

This thesis studied the use of Bayesian Adaptive Spline Surfaces (BASS) as surrogate models within a Bayesian Optimization framework for black-box optimization and hyperparameter tuning. The central objective was to determine whether a BASS-based optimizer can offer competitive sample efficiency when compared with established baselines, while also providing practical flexibility for difficult objective landscapes. To evaluate this, a complete experimental pipeline was developed in R, including multi-dimensional optimization support, reproducible seed-based replication, parallel execution, and standardized convergence reporting against GP-BO and Random Search.

The empirical results show that all methods improve objective values as evaluations accumulate, which confirms that the implementation and evaluation protocol are functioning as intended. In the current completed benchmark setting, GP-BO converges faster than BASS-BO and reaches lower objective values earlier in the budget. Random Search improves more slowly, as expected, and is generally less sample-efficient than both surrogate-guided methods. BASS-BO remains competitive in final performance in several runs, but its average convergence speed is lower than GP-BO on the smooth low-dimensional cases completed so far. This pattern is consistent with the fact that GP surrogates are particularly well matched to smooth, low-dimensional benchmark functions such as Branin-Hoo.

\textbf{A key conclusion of this work is that BASS-BO should not be interpreted as a universally superior replacement for GP-BO.} Instead, it should be seen as a flexible alternative whose performance depends strongly on the optimization regime and on calibration of its control parameters. In this thesis implementation, BASS-BO exposes a richer set of practical tuning knobs than the GP baseline, including dynamic exploration schedules, uncertainty inflation and flooring, local versus global candidate mixing, periodic forced exploration, and surrogate complexity scheduling. These controls are meaningful because they allow the optimizer to adapt its behavior over the course of the search. At the same time, they increase sensitivity to configuration choices, so BASS-BO requires more careful tuning to achieve its best performance.

From a methodological perspective, one contribution of this thesis is the framing of BASS-BO as a configurable optimization system rather than a single fixed algorithm. The experiments demonstrate that decisions about candidate generation, uncertainty calibration, and exploration scheduling have measurable effects on convergence, sometimes as large as the differences between surrogate families themselves. This finding is important for practice, because it implies that fair comparisons between BO methods must hold the full experimental protocol constant, including initialization strategy, candidate pool size, duplicate handling, seed replication, and stopping budget. Without this consistency, measured performance differences can reflect procedure rather than model quality.

The current study also has scope limits. The strongest comparative evidence presently comes from a relatively smalll benchmark set. Because benchmark diversity is limited at this stage, the results should be interpreted as directional rather than universal. In particular, the present experiments emphasize smooth functions where GP assumptions are favorable. The relative advantage of BASS-BO may increase on objectives that are more irregular, nonstationary, or interaction-heavy, and this remains an open empirical question that the established codebase is now prepared to test.

The practical takeaway is therefore balanced. For smooth, low-dimensional objectives under tight evaluation budgets, GP-BO is an excellent default and currently outperforms BASS-BO in convergence speed in this thesis. However, BASS-BO remains a viable method, especially when one expects model mismatch under GP assumptions or when adaptive control of exploration behavior is desirable. In those cases, the additional BASS-BO parameters are not merely cosmetic; they are mechanisms that can change search behavior in ways that may become advantageous on more difficult landscapes.

Future work should extend this analysis in three directions. First, the benchmark suite should be expanded to include more multimodal and higher-dimensional objectives, including noisy settings, so that conclusions are less tied to any one function class. Second, a structured sensitivity study should be completed for the BASS-BO controls, with seed-matched statistical comparisons and effect sizes for both final objective value and convergence-area metrics. Third, systems-level improvements such as asynchronous batch selection and more efficient candidate generation should be explored to reduce runtime overhead and improve practical scalability. Together, these extensions would provide a stronger basis for identifying when BASS-BO is preferable to GP-BO and for recommending robust default configurations.

In summary, this thesis delivers a reproducible and extensible framework for comparing surrogate-based Bayesian Optimization methods, and demonstrates that BASS can be integrated effectively in a multi-dimensional BO loop. The current evidence favors GP-BO on the completed smooth benchmark cases, but it also shows that BASS-BO offers a richer control surface that may be valuable in harder optimization regimes. The main outcome is therefore both empirical and methodological: GP-BO is the stronger baseline in the tested setting, while BASS-BO emerges as a promising, highly tunable alternative whose full potential depends on problem structure and careful calibration.
