\chapter{Experimental Validation}

\section{Introduction}

The previous chapters established the theoretical basis for Bayesian Optimization (BO) with BASS models as surrogates. We discussed initialization challenges, domain discretization, and uncertainty estimation, with special attention to unexplored regions of the search space. The next step is empirical validation.

This chapter presents the experimental framework used to evaluate BASS-based BO for hyperparameter optimization. The goal is to compare BASS against established alternatives and determine where it performs well, where it underperforms, and why. In particular, we study whether BASS can address practical concerns such as cold starts, sample efficiency, and reliable optimization in high-dimensional settings.

The methods compared in this chapter are Grid Search, Random Search, Gaussian Process-based BO (GP-BO), and BASS-based BO (BASS-BO). Experiments are conducted across multiple models, datasets, and synthetic benchmark functions to provide a broad evaluation. The analysis focuses on both optimization quality and computational efficiency.

The chapter is organized as follows. First, we describe the experimental design, including methods, models, datasets, metrics, and statistical procedures. Next, we present and interpret the results. Finally, we summarize the main findings, identify limitations, and provide recommendations for future work.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Objective and Scope}

The primary objective is to evaluate BASS-BO against established hyperparameter optimization methods on two dimensions: optimization effectiveness and optimization efficiency. 

This evaluation criterion is consistent with the central motivation of BO and Sequential Model-Based Optimization, namely to reduce expensive objective function calls while maintaining strong optimization performance. The study includes classical benchmark functions (functions from \cite{simulationlib}) to test behavior under controlled and application-oriented conditions.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization Methods}

The methods compared are the ones previously mentioned in this work along with the new framework woth BASS as the surrogate:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{BASS-Based Bayesian Optimization (BASS-BO)}: the proposed method. BASS surrogates model the response surface and uncertainty, and an acquisition function selects new evaluations iteratively.
	
	\item \textbf{Random Search}: random sampling from predefined hyperparameter distributions. It is often more efficient than Grid Search in moderate to high dimensions, as shown in \cite{bergstra2012random}.
	
	\item \textbf{Gaussian Process-Based Bayesian Optimization (GP-BO)}: a standard BO baseline using Gaussian Processes for surrogate modeling and uncertainty-guided acquisition.
\end{itemize}

The dataset in particular we will be using is the Virtual Library of Simulation Experiments: Test Functions and Datasets \cite{simulationlib}. This suite is composed of standard functions such as the Branin-Hoo function, commonly used to test Bayesian Optimization problems.

\subsection{Experimental Procedure}

Each run follows a common protocol to ensure fair comparison.

\paragraph{1) Initialization}
All methods start from the same initial design generated by random sampling. This reduces cold-start bias and ensures that early information is matched across methods.

\paragraph{2) Iterative optimization}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{BASS-BO}: fit BASS surrogate on observed trials, optimize acquisition function, evaluate selected candidate, update surrogate, repeat.
	\item \textbf{GP-BO}: same loop with GP surrogate.
	\item \textbf{Random Search}: sample and evaluate candidates independently each iteration.
	\item \textbf{Grid Search}: evaluate according to grid ordering until budget is exhausted or grid is completed.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{3) Budget and stopping}
A fixed evaluation budget is used per task to make efficiency comparisons meaningful. When a method terminates early (for example, finite grid exhausted), remaining iterations are treated as no additional improvement.

\paragraph{4) Repetitions and randomization}
Each configuration is repeated across multiple random seeds. Method order and data split assignments are randomized to reduce systematic effects.

\subsection{Evaluation}

As for evaluation of the methods, the central trade-off at the heart of Bayesian Optimization is that we want to achieve great returns at the lowest possible cost. On those axes, we define a performance metric and some efficiency metrics as well to test out our methods. The performance metric is simply the best objective value reached within budget. As for the efficiency metric, we track the evaluations over time to see how the methods behave under different constraints. 

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

To compare methods rigorously, we use confidence intervals to make sure we have a wide understanding of the behavior of the methods themselves, as well as effect sizes to quantify practical significance. 

\subsection{Initial Testing Conditions}

The initial testing conditions used in this study are listed below. Full implementation details, including scripts, targets, and output files, are available in the project repository: \url{github.com/AdrianTJ/BayesianOptim_BASS}.

\begin{itemize}
	\item Branin-Hoo function ($2d$), bounded domain)
	\item Rasteguin ($4d$, bounded domain)
	\item Additional benchmark functions (to be added after completion of all runs)
\end{itemize}

All methods are compared under a fixed evaluation budget and identical initialization strategy. For each replicate, an initial design of size $n_0=\max(2d+1,8)$ is generated via maximin Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) where $d$ is the dimension of the particular problem, and each optimizer proceeds for a fixed number of iterations. Results are aggregated over multiple random seeds (in our testing, 25 of them), and convergence is summarized using the best-so-far objective value at each iteration. Particular care was taken for these function evaluations to ensure reproducibility, so the set seed alsways starts at the default value of 1001, and proceeds to seed 1026. 

\subsection{BASS-BO Hyperparameters and Practical Tuning Space}

A central advantage of the proposed BASS-BO framework is that it exposes several tunable controls that directly affect exploration, exploitation, uncertainty calibration, and local refinement. In contrast to a more rigid surrogate setup, these controls allow the optimizer to adapt its behavior to the objective landscape and the available evaluation budget. In the current implementation, the most relevant BASS-BO parameters are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{bass\_kappa\_start}, \texttt{bass\_kappa\_end}: define the exploration schedule in the lower confidence bound (LCB) acquisition rule. A larger early value encourages broader search, while a smaller late value emphasizes refinement.
	
	\item \texttt{n\_cand}: number of candidate points sampled per iteration for acquisition maximization/minimization. Larger values improve candidate quality but increase computation.
	
	\item \texttt{bass\_local\_frac}: fraction of candidates sampled locally around the current best point. Higher values increase local exploitation.
	
	\item \texttt{bass\_local\_sd}: local sampling spread around the current best point in normalized coordinates. Larger values widen local search neighborhoods.
	
	\item \texttt{bass\_explore\_every}: frequency of forced exploration steps. At this interval, candidate selection prioritizes posterior uncertainty rather than LCB score.
	
	\item \texttt{bass\_degree\_early}, \texttt{bass\_degree\_late}, \texttt{bass\_switch\_after}: control surrogate complexity over time. Lower early degree can improve stability with limited data, while higher late degree increases expressiveness once more observations are available.
	
	\item \texttt{bass\_sd\_inflate}: multiplicative inflation factor applied to posterior standard deviation for acquisition. This counteracts early overconfidence and increases exploratory behavior.
	
	\item \texttt{bass\_sd\_floor}: minimum uncertainty floor to prevent near-zero posterior variance from collapsing exploration.
\end{itemize}

These parameters make BASS-BO highly configurable. The same flexibility is also a source of sensitivity, since poor settings can degrade convergence and it can become easy to shoot oneself in the foot with so much freedom. For this reason, we treat parameter selection as part of the method design, and report both the defaults and tuned settings used in each experiment. All reported numbers are with the default parameter set in the code, presented below. 

\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{bass\_kappa\_start} $= 3.5$
	\item \texttt{bass\_kappa\_end} $= 1.5$
	\item \texttt{bass\_sd\_floor} $= 10^{-3}$
	\item \texttt{bass\_sd\_inflate} $= 1.20$
	\item \texttt{bass\_local\_frac} $= 0.35$
	\item \texttt{bass\_local\_sd} $= 0.08$
	\item \texttt{bass\_explore\_every} $= 7$
	\item \texttt{bass\_degree\_early} $= 1$
	\item \texttt{bass\_degree\_late} $= 2$
	\item \texttt{bass\_switch\_after} $= 40$
\end{itemize}

This configuration is intended to provide strong early exploration, gradual transition to exploitation, and robust behavior against early uncertainty collapse.

\subsection{Interpretation of BASS-BO Controls}

The BASS-BO controls can be grouped into four functional categories:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Exploration--Exploitation Controls}:
	\texttt{bass\_kappa\_start}, \texttt{bass\_kappa\_end}, and \texttt{bass\_explore\_every}. These govern how aggressively the method explores uncertain regions versus exploiting known promising areas.
	
	\item \textbf{Candidate-Set Design Controls}:
	\texttt{n\_cand}, \texttt{bass\_local\_frac}, and \texttt{bass\_local\_sd}. These determine whether search is globally diverse, locally concentrated, or hybrid.
	
	\item \textbf{Surrogate Complexity Controls}:
	\texttt{bass\_degree\_early}, \texttt{bass\_degree\_late}, and \texttt{bass\_switch\_after}. These control how quickly model complexity increases as data accumulates.
	
	\item \textbf{Uncertainty Calibration Controls}:
	\texttt{bass\_sd\_inflate} and \texttt{bass\_sd\_floor}. These reduce overconfident behavior and improve robustness in early iterations.
\end{enumerate}

Empirically, these groups interact. For example, increasing \texttt{n\_cand} without revisiting \texttt{bass\_kappa\_*} may not yield better search behavior, and adding strong local sampling (\texttt{bass\_local\_frac}) can require stronger uncertainty inflation (\texttt{bass\_sd\_inflate}) to avoid premature convergence. An important type of test that should be run with these types of parameters is sensitivity analysis, but that is outside the reach of this current project.

\subsection{Rastrigin Function}

\begin{equation}
	f(\mathbf{x}) = A n + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[x_i^2 - A\cos\left(2\pi x_i\right)\right],
\end{equation}
\[
\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n), \qquad x_i \in [-5.12,\,5.12], \quad i=1,\dots,n,
\]
where \(A=10\) is the standard choice. We used the 4 dimensional version of this function.
