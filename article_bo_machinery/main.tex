\begin{abstract}
Comparisons of surrogate models for Bayesian optimization (BO) over mixed and
categorical search spaces are usually read as statements about the surrogates.
We show that they are frequently statements about the surrounding
\emph{machinery} instead: the candidate-generation scheme that the acquisition
function is optimized over, and the duplicate handling that decides which
proposals are admissible. Using a controlled pipeline in which two structurally
different surrogates (a Gaussian process over a continuous relaxation, and a
Bayesian adaptive spline surface with native categorical support) share one
acquisition rule and one candidate generator, we demonstrate two failure modes.
First, a single degree of freedom in the local proposal distribution ---
whether a candidate may retain the incumbent's categorical combination while
refining its continuous coordinates --- places a hard ceiling on \emph{every}
surrogate: in oracle experiments that replace the acquisition with the true
objective, the restricted generator loses to the permissive one on every
paired seed of two standard mixed benchmarks, and no surrogate, however good,
can recover the gap. Second, duplicate handling that compares raw encodings
rather than decoded categorical combinations silently re-spends a majority of
the evaluation budget on already-known points. Both effects are invisible in
standard convergence plots and are readily misattributed to the surrogate. We
propose the \emph{oracle-ceiling audit} --- upper-bounding attainable
performance under a perfect surrogate --- as a cheap, general diagnostic for
BO pipelines, together with a checklist for machinery-controlled surrogate
comparisons: shared schema-aware proposals, combination-level deduplication,
paired seeds, and per-seed win statistics.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

% Argument: (1) BO quality is usually attributed to the surrogate; (2) in
% mixed/categorical spaces the acquisition cannot be optimized in closed form,
% so every method ships bespoke search machinery; (3) that machinery is rarely
% shared, audited, or even fully described; (4) we show it can dominate
% outcomes; (5) contributions.

The sample efficiency of Bayesian optimization is conventionally credited to
its surrogate model, and comparative studies of surrogates --- Gaussian
processes, random forests, density-ratio methods, spline models --- are read
as evidence about which model class best captures the objective
\cite{Snoek_Larochelle_Adams_2012, bergstra2011algorithms}. Over continuous
domains this attribution is at least plausible: the acquisition function can
be optimized to near-optimality with standard multi-start gradient methods, so
the surrogate is the main thing that differs between pipelines. Over mixed and
categorical domains the situation is different. The acquisition must be
optimized over a discrete or hybrid space, every method ships its own
machinery for doing so --- candidate pools, local search, trust regions,
bandit layers \cite{hutter2011sequential, ru2020cocabo, wan2021think} --- and
that machinery is typically inseparable from the method being evaluated.

% TODO: 1-2 paragraphs sharpening the claim with the two concrete failure
% modes (generator ceiling, duplicate leakage) and preview of the oracle audit.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The oracle-ceiling audit.} Replacing the acquisition score
  with the true objective turns the BO loop into an oracle-guided search whose
  performance upper-bounds what \emph{any} surrogate could achieve with the
  same machinery. Comparing this ceiling across machinery variants isolates
  machinery effects at the cost of a few cheap runs, with no surrogate fits.
  \item \textbf{Two demonstrated failure modes.} A restricted local proposal
  distribution (all local candidates forced to change the incumbent's
  categorical combination) caps the oracle itself well short of the optimum on
  the CoCaBO mixed benchmarks; encoding-level duplicate detection lets a
  majority of the budget be re-spent on known categorical combinations. Both
  are invisible in convergence plots.
  \item \textbf{A machinery-controlled comparison protocol.} Shared
  schema-aware candidate generation, combination-level deduplication, paired
  per-seed designs, and win/tie/loss reporting with signed-rank tests; we show
  the same two surrogates change their apparent ranking on a mixed benchmark
  when the machinery is repaired, under an identical acquisition rule.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% =============================================================================

% Position: we do not propose new machinery; the machinery pieces are standard.
% The contribution is the audit + the demonstration that machinery choices
% confound surrogate attribution, which the literature largely leaves implicit.

\paragraph{Mixed and categorical BO.} SMAC optimizes a random-forest
surrogate's acquisition by local search over configuration neighborhoods
(one-exchange categorical flips) interleaved with random candidates
\cite{hutter2011sequential}. TPE models densities over good and bad trials and
handles categoricals natively \cite{bergstra2011algorithms, watanabe2023tree,
akiba2019optuna}. CoCaBO couples a multi-armed bandit over categorical
combinations with a GP over the continuous subspace \cite{ru2020cocabo};
Casmopolitan builds Hamming-distance trust regions around the incumbent
\cite{wan2021think}; COMBO works over combinatorial graphs \cite{oh2019combo}.
For GPs specifically, the baseline treatment of categorical inputs is a
continuous relaxation, formalized (with a covariance transformation) by
\citet{garrido2020dealing}. Spline-based surrogates with native factor support
descend from MARS and Bayesian MARS \cite{friedman1991multivariate,
denison1998bayesian}, with BASS as the modern implementation
\cite{francom2020bass}.
